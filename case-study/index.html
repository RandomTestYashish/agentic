The problem

People open a telco app with one job in mind. The app makes them hunt through menus to do it, so many give up and call the care centre. Every one of those calls costs money and goodwill.

What I did

Led design end to end: reframed navigation as intent, set principles for an agent people can trust, designed the conversation model, widgets, voice and motion, and built this working prototype.

What it changes

A task like a data top-up goes from seven taps across four screens to one sentence and a confirm. The north star we set: tasks completed inside the chat, fewer digital-to-assisted hand-offs.

My role

Hands on the pixels, and in every room.

I owned the experience from the first framing doc to the motion spec, and kept product, engineering and data pointed at the same picture.

The problem

Navigation assumes people explore. They arrive with intent.

Telco app sessions are short and transactional. Nobody browses their prepaid plan. Yet a person with a clear goal still has to:

Search through menusEntry points live three levels deep, under labels that match the org chart, not the question.
Complete many stepsSimple jobs like a top-up or a bill query stretch across several screens.
Fall back to a humanWhen the path isn’t obvious, people call or visit a store. That is the digital-to-assisted (D2A) leak.

Design decisions

Twelve calls, and why I made them.

The phone follows along as you read. Each decision lists what I chose, the reasoning behind it, and the trade-off I accepted.

01Assist when it matters

Put the entry where intent forms: the header.

Decision
The entry point is a pill in the top bar that cycles real requests, not a floating button or a new tab.
Why
People decide what they came to do in the first second, at the top of the screen. A rotating prompt teaches capability without a tutorial. A FAB competes with bottom nav and gets read as chat support.
Trade-off
It takes space from the search slot. We kept search reachable inside the conversation, so nothing was lost.
02Transparent

Agree on the rules before the first answer.

Decision
A one-screen first run: what the assistant is good at, that it is still learning, and what never to share. One “I Agree”.
Why
Setting expectations early lowers the cost of a wrong answer later. It also covers consent for account access, which legal needed anyway.
Trade-off
One extra tap on first use. It only shows once, and the pill opens straight into it so it reads as part of the moment, not a gate.
03Assist when it matters

A warm greeting that steps aside as soon as you type.

Decision
The orb and “Hey Yashish” sit centred on arrival, then glide to the top the moment the first character is typed.
Why
The centre says “I’m listening”. Once someone starts typing, the keyboard and their words need the room. Docking on keystroke, not on focus, avoids a jump when people just tap around.
Trade-off
No starter chips on landing. The pill already showed examples, and chips here pulled attention from typing.
04Transparent

Show the work while it thinks.

Decision
A mini orb spins while the status line narrates real steps: “Thinking about it…”, then “Checking current usage…”, then “Reading today’s data meter…”.
Why
A spinner says “wait”. A narrated step says “I’m doing the thing you asked”, which makes the same wait feel shorter and makes the answer feel earned.
Trade-off
Steps must reflect what the backend actually does. We agreed with engineering to emit real stage events, never decorative ones.
05Assist when it matters

Answers are widgets, not paragraphs.

Decision
Anything with numbers renders as a card from the app’s own design system: usage meters, packs, plans, bills, each with its action inside.
Why
“1.1 GB of 2 GB left” is faster to read as a bar than as a sentence, and a button inside the answer turns reading into doing. Reusing system cards meant engineering could ship them as-is.
Trade-off
Less freedom than free text. We kept a short sentence above every card, so the answer still reads well aloud.
06Transparent

Recommend one, and say why.

Decision
One “Best match” pack is shown first with a reason (“you use about 1.9 GB a day”), and two alternatives sit below it.
Why
A wall of twelve packs is the old app again. One explained pick reads as advice, not a sales pitch, and keeps choice for anyone who disagrees.
Trade-off
Business wanted the premium pack first. We agreed the pick follows usage, with premium shown as an alternative.
07You stay in control

Money never moves without a confirm.

Decision
Every purchase opens a sheet with the amount, the item, the number and the payment method. Pay is a separate, deliberate tap.
Why
An agent that can spend is only trusted if it always asks. The sheet keeps people in the chat, so the flow doesn’t break into a checkout.
Trade-off
One more tap than a one-click buy. Worth it: an accidental charge would cost far more trust than the tap saves.
08Fail gracefully

Diagnose first. Escalate with context.

Decision
“My internet is slow” runs a visible check (outage, SIM, signal, speed), explains the cause, gives fixes in order, then offers a complaint with location already attached.
Why
Most slow-net complaints have a cause the person can fix in a minute. Showing the check builds belief in the fix, and when it can’t help, the ticket arrives pre-filled, so nobody repeats themselves.
Trade-off
The check takes about three seconds. We made each row resolve one by one so the wait has rhythm.
09Assist when it matters

Voice lives in the composer, not a separate mode.

Decision
Tapping the mic turns the composer into a live waveform with a transcript above it. Stop, see “Transcribing…”, and it sends.
Why
A full-screen voice mode is a context switch. Keeping it in place keeps the conversation the hero, and the live transcript lets people catch a mis-hear before it sends. Hinglish works too.
Trade-off
Less room for a big visualiser. A full voice mode still exists, one tap away on the orb, for hands-free use.
10Transparent

Two tones: the AI is ember, actions are the app.

Decision
Ember marks the AI (pill, orb, voice). Cards and buttons inside answers keep the app’s own language: black CTAs, blue links.
Why
The AI should feel new. Paying a bill should feel exactly as safe as it always did. Splitting the palette tells people which parts are suggestion and which are the bank-grade action.
Trade-off
Two palettes need discipline. I documented where ember is allowed, so the split survives handover.
11Fail gracefully

When it can’t help, say what it can do.

Decision
Out-of-scope requests get an honest “I’m still learning that”, followed by the five things it can do right now, as tappable follow-ups.
Why
A confident wrong answer is the fastest way to lose trust in an AI. An honest boundary plus a next step keeps people moving instead of calling care.
Trade-off
It admits limits openly. That’s the point: limits are fine, surprises are not.
12Identity

One object, many states: the orb.

Decision
A single orb is the assistant’s body. It grows out of the pill, bounces into place, docks when you type, spins while thinking, and reacts to your voice.
Why
People read one consistent character faster than a set of loaders and icons. State changes on the same object feel like the assistant reacting, not the UI changing.
Trade-off
Motion has to be cheap. Every move is transform-only and spring-driven, so it holds 60 fps on mid-range Android.

Capabilities framework

Every conversation has five beats.

I mapped what the assistant must be able to do at each stage, so product and engineering could plan capability by capability instead of feature by feature.

Chat history48-hour conversational context, 90-day memory, and session continuity across app restarts.
Nudges & presetsSmart recommendations, personalised prompts and contextual shortcuts on the pill.
Multi-modalityText and voice today. Images, attachments and video diagnostics on the roadmap.
Motion & craft

Physics, not keyframes.

Early transitions felt boxy: speed jumped between steps. I rebuilt them on one damped spring per motion, transform and opacity only. Play with the same spring the prototype uses.

The orb’s landing spring

After “I Agree”, the orb is driven by an under-damped spring, so it overshoots and settles. The dashed ring is a linear ease for comparison.

What I learned

The most useful thing I designed was clarity.

In a space full of ambiguity, design set the vision, aligned stakeholders, and turned a broad AI ambition into something the team could build.

Prototype the feeling, early

Static frames couldn’t settle debates about pace and trust. A working prototype did, in one review.

Design the system, not the screen

Agreeing on the conversation model with engineering decided half the UI before I drew it.

Trust is a budget

Every surprise spends it. Confirms, honest limits and visible work refill it.

How we measure it

  • Task completion rate inside the chat
  • Digital-to-assisted hand-offs per 1,000 sessions
  • Fallback rate and recovery after fallback
  • Answer feedback (thumbs) and post-chat rating

What’s next
